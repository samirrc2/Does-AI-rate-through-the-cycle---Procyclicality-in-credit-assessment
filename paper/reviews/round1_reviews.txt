
Reviewer #1: This manuscript investigates whether large language models exhibit procyclical behavior in credit assessment. The authors construct synthetic firms with fixed fundamentals and vary only the macroeconomic environment across five ordered states. Five production-tier LLMs are evaluated under different prompt framings, with a credit-irrelevant placebo condition used as an additional control. The study addresses a timely and relevant question at the intersection of artificial intelligence, credit risk, and financial regulation. The controlled experimental design is interesting, and the results provide potentially useful evidence. However, several issues should be addressed to further strengthen the reliability and interpretation of the findings.

1. The study relies entirely on 80 synthetic firms. Although this design provides clean identification by holding firm fundamentals fixed, it remains unclear whether the observed procyclicality generalizes to real-world corporate credit assessment. The authors should provide additional validation using real firms or historical financial statements, or more clearly discuss the external validity of the synthetic experimental setting.

2. The construction of the 80 synthetic firms requires further justification. The manuscript states that they cover five credit-quality tiers and eight sectors, but provides limited information on how their financial characteristics were generated and calibrated. The authors should clarify whether the distributions and combinations of leverage, profitability, liquidity, interest coverage, and other variables are representative of actual firms.

3. The five macroeconomic states are manually constructed using GDP growth, unemployment, credit spreads, default rates, and lending standards. However, these indicators change simultaneously and monotonically across the severity axis. Therefore, the current experiment cannot determine which macroeconomic signals primarily drive the observed rating changes. Additional experiments varying individual macroeconomic factors would help identify the sources of LLM procyclicality.

4. The comparison across models should be interpreted more cautiously. The five models come from only two model families, and several are "mini", "Flash", or "Lite" variants. Table 1 shows substantial differences across models, with the procyclicality coefficient ranging from 0.230 to 0.775. Expanding the evaluation to additional model families or stronger frontier models would provide more convincing support for claims concerning LLM-based credit assessment more generally. They can see these recent works: Two-Channel Graph Inference and Matrix Completion for Predicting CircRNA-Disease Associations in Consumer Health，A rapid prediction model for MiRNA-LncRNA interactions utilizing multi-view projection fusion and random parallel matrix factorization and Higher-Order Dynamic Disentangled Intent Sensing and Bidirectional Joint Updating Framework for NcRNA-Drug Resistance Association Prediction.

5. The through-the-cycle prompting experiment is interesting, but the interpretation that the instruction "anchors rather than de-cyclifies" the models requires stronger validation. The conclusion mainly relies on the reduction in the verbalized one-year PD slope. Since LLM-generated probabilities may themselves be poorly calibrated, additional calibration analysis or alternative measures of point-in-time risk sensitivity would strengthen this conclusion.

6. The capital-consequence analysis in Section 4.4 relies on a stylized mapping from LLM-generated rating notches to default probabilities and then to Basel IRB capital requirements. Although the authors acknowledge that this is a stylized proxy, the resulting capital changes may depend considerably on the assumed rating-to-PD mapping. A more comprehensive sensitivity analysis using alternative mappings or parameter settings would improve the robustness of this economic interpretation.

7. The experimental design uses two runs per condition at temperature zero. Although the authors report a within-cell noise analysis, LLM outputs may still vary across repeated API calls and model updates. Increasing the number of repeated runs and reporting the stability of the main findings across repetitions would provide stronger evidence regarding the robustness of the reported effects.

8. Some conclusions should be moderated to reflect the scope of the experiment. The study provides evidence that the tested LLMs exhibit procyclical behavior under controlled synthetic scenarios, but it does not directly evaluate deployed credit-rating systems or actual lending decisions. Therefore, broader statements regarding the prudential consequences of "LLM-based credit assessment" should be clearly distinguished from the experimentally demonstrated findings.


Reviewer #2: I appreciate this opportunity to review this manuscript, which seems to be a creative addition to the literature investigating LLMs in finance. While I appreciate the work, I have two main issues that the Authors should think about while revising their manuscript.

The first issue is that is extremely well-known from research on LLMs that their capabilities might substantially differ depending on which class of model is used, with work in this journal showing that there was an enormous shift in the quality of output between older/less capable models and newer/more capable models. For example, the financial literacy of GPT models has substantially increased between GPT-3.5 and GPT-4. GPT-6 Astra, for instance, is considerably more capable than GPT-4o (one of the five models used in this study). In fact, none of the models investigated in the study - Gemini Flash, Gemini Flash-Lite, GPT-4.1-mini, GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini - is a frontier model (three are mini or lite variants). The main point I am trying to make here is that results might be substantially different if one was to use more advanced models, for example GPT-5 Pro, or now GPT-6 Astra. For Anthropic models, this would consistute the Opus or Fable models. Of course, I am cognizant that these models are much more expensive than the models that have been used in the study, but I would argue that if one would conduct these types of analyzes (concerning credit ratings), they would be more inclined to actually use the more sophisticated (and expensive) models. Therefore, I would suggest that the Authors see whether the findings remain similar when using a state-of-the-art model, rather than the rather "pedestrian" models that were used here.

The second issue is somewhat related to the first one. Why might someone use LLMs to conduct credit assessments? People can of course ask any type of question to LLMs, but is it plausible that someone would actually rely on such assessments? It is extremely unlikely that rating agencies would use simple, unsophisticated models when constructing ratings, but maybe there is a case for retail investors using them to inform them on their investment decisions? The Authors should try a little bit harder to convince the reader that this use-case is actually plausible. I think that the less sophisticated the model, the more implausible it is that LLMs present any use in such tasks.

Minor issues:
- The Authors use temperature = 0 as a setting for their LLMs. This should be justified (higher temperature settings might produce more variable, yet plausible results; the CIs are pretty narrow, perhaps too narrow).
- I think that writing what is and isn't suitable for a specific journal is appropriate for a cover letter, but not for the manuscript. Therefore "for which this journal has become a leading outlet" should be removed.

Best wishes to the Authors.


Reviewer #3: The paper asks whether LLM-based credit assessments rate "through the cycle" or instead amplify the macroeconomic cycle. The authors hold synthetic firm fundamentals byte-identical across an ordered five-point macroeconomic-severity axis and a matched credit-irrelevant placebo axis, and elicit letter ratings and one-year default probabilities from five production-tier models under four prompt framings. The paper's contribution is clear and important, but following issues should be discussed:
1. Define the through-the-cycle benchmark more precisely. The paper correctly states that genuine TTC behaviour is a joint condition: the rating slope should fall toward zero while the point-in-time PD should remain sensitive. Please clarify what "toward zero" and "near one" mean in practice. TTC ratings need not be perfectly flat, as the rating literature recognizes.
2. Add formal tests for model and framing differences. The paper emphasizes a roughly threefold spread across models and a large difference between TTC and "be stable" framings. Please provide formal pairwise tests or bootstrap confidence intervals for these differences. Overlapping per-model CIs do not by themselves establish or rule out significant differences.
﻿3. Explain the placebo-adjusted net effect more fully. The placebo moves ratings by 0.042 notches per step, and the net effect is 0.387. Please state whether the net effect is computed by subtracting slopes, by a joint regression with both axes, or by another method.
4. Table 1 should define "D:U," "Resid.," and "PD-ret." in the notes. Table 3 should explain why the expected-notch slope is blank for the Gemini models and why the noise SD is 0.000 for Gemini Flash-Lite. "Parsing at 1.000" should be written as 100% or 1.00 for clarity. Figure 2 should include confidence intervals or clearly state if it does not.
