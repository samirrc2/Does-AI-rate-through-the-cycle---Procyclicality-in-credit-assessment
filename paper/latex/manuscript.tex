\begin{abstract}
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly proposed as credit-rating and default-assessment
tools, yet whether they rate ``through the cycle'' or amplify it---a property prudential regulators
care about but that has been asserted rather than measured---has remained untested. We hold
synthetic firm fundamentals byte-identical across an ordered five-point macroeconomic-severity axis
(plus a matched credit-irrelevant placebo axis) and elicit letter ratings and one-year default
probabilities from five production-tier models (two families, two capability tiers), so that any rating movement is procyclicality by
construction, identified without the unobserved-fundamentals confound that limits the classical
Amato--Furfine test. Across 32{,}000 ratings, every model is significantly procyclical (pooled
$0.429$ rating notches of downgrade per severity step, 95\% CI $[0.41,0.45]$; a $1.76$-notch
boom-to-recession swing), the effect is sharply asymmetric ($3.2\times$ steeper into recessions than
out of them), an order of magnitude larger than the placebo, and robust to model-version fixed
effects and a token-log\-probability estimator. An explicit ``through-the-cycle'' instruction lowers
the rating slope but does not restore through-the-cycle behaviour---it anchors the rating while
suppressing the point-in-time default probability that should move, whereas a naive ``be stable''
instruction preserves that distinction better. Because model choice and prompt framing are
first-order determinants of the effect, LLM-based credit assessment can silently import the
capital-procyclicality channel that regulation seeks to dampen.
\end{abstract}

\begin{highlights}
\item First controlled test of procyclicality in LLM credit ratings.
\item All five production-tier LLMs downgrade identical firms as the macro worsens.
\item Pooled effect: 0.43 rating notches of downgrade per macro-severity step.
\item The effect is asymmetric: about 3x steeper into recessions than booms.
\item A ``through-the-cycle'' prompt anchors ratings rather than de-cyclifying them.
\end{highlights}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
Credit ratings are important for debt pricing, investment decisions, and regulatory capital
requirements. A perennial concern is that ratings could be procyclical, with companies downgraded
during recessions and upgraded during expansions even when the underlying credit fundamentals have not
materially changed. Such rating changes can exacerbate the credit cycle by raising borrowing costs and,
through internal-ratings-based (IRB) capital rules, capital requirements when economic conditions are
already weak \citep{kashyap2004,gordy2006}. Rating agencies therefore attempt to capture
creditworthiness ``through the cycle'' (TTC), placing more importance on a borrower's ability to
withstand temporary macroeconomic fluctuations than on mechanically reacting to current conditions
\citep{amato2004,loffler2004}. However, whether these ratings actually serve this purpose remains
debated \citep{loffler2013}.

There is growing interest in using large language models (LLMs) for financial decision-making,
including credit decisioning and risk assessment, and supervisors have flagged that generative models
could re-introduce procyclicality \citep{bis2024}. This concern is justified: LLMs behave as
context-sensitive economic agents \citep{horton2023,binz2023}, their outputs are highly sensitive to
the way prompts are framed \citep{tversky1981}, and a shared foundation model could homogenise
decisions across users \citep{bommasani2022}. Yet the size of this effect on credit ratings has not
been directly measured. Identifying procyclicality in observational studies of human agencies is
difficult because firm fundamentals and macroeconomic conditions tend to move together, so the
classical ordered-probit test of \citet{amato2004} must proxy for unobserved fundamentals. Recent
evidence that frontier LLMs reproduce date-specific human economic sentiment \citep{graham2026} makes
the question both timely and concrete.

This paper uses a controlled experimental setting to test the procyclicality of LLM-based credit
rating. We keep the firm fundamentals unchanged and only change the macroeconomic environment, so any
systematic change in the assigned rating can be attributed to the macroeconomic context and not to
changes in firm credit quality. The benchmark is not that any movement is bad: a genuine
through-the-cycle rater should hold the \emph{rating} steady while its point-in-time default
probability still tracks conditions, and we test that joint condition directly. We make three
contributions. We first show that all five production-tier models tested downgrade identical firms as
the state of the economy deteriorates, but the magnitude differs substantially across models. Second,
we find a strong asymmetry, with rating sensitivity significantly larger in recessions than in
expansions. Third, we test whether this behaviour can be mitigated via prompt design: an explicit
through-the-cycle instruction decreases rating movement, but it also suppresses the one-year default
probability's responsiveness, suggesting that the model anchors its outputs instead of distinguishing
between point-in-time risk and through-the-cycle ratings \citep{tversky1974}. Section~\ref{sec:lit}
positions the contribution; Section~\ref{sec:design} sets out the design; Section~\ref{sec:results}
reports the results; Section~\ref{sec:concl} concludes.

\section{Related literature}\label{sec:lit}
Our study relates to three strands of literature. The first relates to the assessment of
through-the-cycle and rating procyclicality. \citet{amato2004} test the response of credit ratings to
the business cycle, controlling for firm fundamentals, and \citet{loffler2004,loffler2013} examine the
extent to which rating agencies achieve through-the-cycle stability. The prudential stakes are set by
the interaction of ratings with Basel capital rules \citep{kashyap2004,gordy2006}, with sovereign and
corporate ratings alike carrying real pricing consequences \citep{cantor1996}.

The second strand is the rapid adoption of \emph{LLMs in finance}, for which this journal has become a
leading outlet \citep{ali2025}. Recent applications include return prediction \citep{lopezlira2023},
financial-statement analysis \citep{kim2024}, financial advice \citep{fieberg2025,kirschbaum2026}, and
use as simulated economic agents \citep{horton2023}; within finance specifically, models have been
turned to measuring monetary-policy uncertainty \citep{ito2025}, assessing the financial conditions of
AI firms \citep{giaretta2026}, and---most relevant here---to reliability evidence in banking
supervision \citep{garcia2026}. A parallel line warns that machine-generated scores and disclosures
can mislead, from greenwashing in ESG scores \citep{kathan2025} to ``AI washing'' in corporate
disclosure \citep{song2026}, while broader analyses consider AI's systemic and macroeconomic
footprint, from agent-based market (in)stability \citep{llacay2026} to the skill premium
\citep{bloom2025}. Most closely, \citet{hens2025} compare the risk profiles LLMs produce for investors
with those of experienced bankers, finding relatively small differences in the levels of risk
assigned, although the models give weaker explanations. We examine a different dimension of
reliability---whether an LLM alters its credit assessment in response to economically irrelevant
changes in the macroeconomic context surrounding otherwise identical firm information.

The third strand is the susceptibility of LLM outputs to framing and contextual information. Prior
work documents anchoring, framing effects, and imperfect calibration in language-model decisions
\citep{tversky1974,tversky1981,binz2023,kadavath2022}, and shows that shared foundation models can
homogenise decisions across users \citep{bommasani2022}. In our setting these behavioural properties
connect to a specific financial mechanism: if the macroeconomic context systematically affects credit
ratings even when the underlying firm fundamentals do not change, contextual sensitivity translates
directly into rating procyclicality.

\section{Design and identification}\label{sec:design}
The unit of analysis is a rating cell $=$ (model, prompt framing, seed, firm, macro state). We
construct $80$ synthetic firms spanning five credit-quality tiers and eight sectors, and characterise
each firm using the same set of credit fundamentals: revenue growth, EBITDA margin, leverage, interest
coverage, free cash flow, liquidity, and Altman-style financial ratios \citep{altman1968}. The
information about each firm presented to the models is the same in all experimental conditions; each
firm's fundamentals are serialised once and reused byte-identically under every macro state. Following
this, we merely alter the model's exposure to the macroeconomic environment by switching it between
five ordered states---boom, expansion, neutral, slowdown, and severe recession (severity
$-2\ldots+2$). A common set of macroeconomic indicators is used to represent each state, including GDP
growth, unemployment, credit spreads, default rates, and lending standards. No calendar dates are
given, reducing the chance that a model associates a situation with a particular historical event.

To strengthen identification, we add two controls. We first generate a matched placebo condition
carrying credit-irrelevant information about regional weather and traffic. The placebo has the same
format and a similar amount of numerical information as the macroeconomic scenarios but should not
affect a firm's creditworthiness; it helps us differentiate macroeconomic sensitivity from a more
general tendency to respond to changing contextual information, and the net (macro minus placebo)
coefficient is the demand-effect-adjusted cyclicality. Second, we keep the same random seed for a
given firm and prompt framing across all macroeconomic states, so that sampling variation cannot be
misinterpreted as a cyclical effect. Each assessment produces a letter credit rating (mapped to an
S\&P notch on a 21-point scale) and a one-year probability of default (PD).

We consider four prompt framings: a terse baseline, a point-in-time instruction, an explicit
through-the-cycle instruction, and a generic ``be stable'' instruction that makes no reference to the
credit cycle. The experiment uses five production-tier models from two families---three from OpenAI
(\texttt{gpt-4o-mini}, \texttt{gpt-4.1-mini}, \texttt{gpt-4o}) and two Google Gemini Flash-tier models
accessed via the \texttt{gemini-flash-latest} and \texttt{gemini-flash-lite-latest} aliases---all
queried in July 2026 with the served-model snapshot (\texttt{system\_fingerprint}) logged per call for
reproducibility. For each condition each model is run twice at temperature zero. Our main dependent
variable is the change in rating notch for a firm as macroeconomic severity increases, with a positive
coefficient implying greater procyclicality. We report average firm slopes and 95\% confidence
intervals based on $2{,}000$ firm-clustered, tier-stratified bootstrap draws. We also look at the total
boom-to-recession rating swing, the difference in downside and upside sensitivity, the Amato--Furfine
ordered-probit coefficient, and---for the OpenAI models---a continuous expected-notch slope from the
rating-token log-probabilities. The whole experiment yields $32{,}000$ credit assessments for
US\$$13.69$, with zero parse errors.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
\subsection{LLMs are procyclical on identical fundamentals}\label{sec:pro}
On byte-identical fundamentals, all five models downgrade the same firms as the described macroeconomy
worsens (Table~\ref{tab:permodel}; Fig.~\ref{fig:main}a). Pooling across models under the terse
baseline, ratings fall by $0.429$ notches per severity step (95\% CI $[0.41,0.45]$)---a $1.76$-notch
swing from boom to severe recession, enough to move a BBB firm toward BB$+$/BB with nothing real
changing. The per-model coefficient ranges from $0.230$ for Gemini Flash to $0.775$ for GPT-4o-mini,
and no model's confidence interval contains zero, so the choice of model is itself a first-order
determinant of procyclicality---a roughly threefold spread. Among the OpenAI models, GPT-4o is
markedly less sensitive than GPT-4o-mini, with slopes of $0.387$ and $0.775$ respectively.

The result is robust across several checks, detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:robust}: the matched
credit-irrelevant placebo moves ratings only $0.042$ notches per step, so the net macro-minus-placebo
effect is $0.387$ (95\% CI $[0.36,0.41]$), still excluding zero, and the coefficient survives
served-model fixed effects, a rating-token log-probability estimator, and the Amato--Furfine
ordered-probit null (Table~\ref{tab:robust}).

The magnitudes are economically material. A pooled $1.76$-notch swing means that a firm with unchanged
fundamentals---rated, say, A$-$ in a boom---can be rated near BBB in a severe recession purely because
of the macroeconomic environment in which the model is placed, and for the most sensitive model the
boom-to-recession swing exceeds three notches ($\S$\ref{sec:capital}).

\subsection{The procyclicality is asymmetric}\label{sec:asym}
The response to macroeconomic conditions is highly asymmetric. Splitting the macro axis at neutral,
the pooled downside half-slope (neutral to severe recession) is $0.67$ notches per step (95\% CI
$[0.63,0.72]$) versus an upside half-slope (boom to neutral) of $0.21$ (95\% CI $[0.18,0.24]$), so
downgrade sensitivity is about $3.2$ times larger than upgrade sensitivity, a pattern seen in all five
models (Fig.~\ref{fig:asym}). This asymmetry suggests that LLM ratings are
more sensitive to deteriorating than to improving conditions, and it matters because downgrades in
downturns raise borrowing costs and capital requirements exactly when financial conditions are already
under strain; it also echoes the stricter-in-downturns behaviour long attributed to human agencies
\citep{loffler2004}. Because capital requirements are convex in the rating and bind hardest in stress,
an asymmetric downgrade profile is more procyclical than a symmetric one of equal average slope.

\subsection{``Through-the-cycle'' instructions anchor rather than de-cyclify}\label{sec:ttc}
Prompt framing is a strong determinant of rating sensitivity: the estimated slope declines from
$0.516$ for the point-in-time instruction to $0.429$ for the terse baseline, $0.329$ for the ``be
stable'' instruction, and $0.160$ for the explicit through-the-cycle instruction (95\% CI
$[0.14,0.18]$; terse-minus-TTC contrast $0.269$). At first sight this suggests that through-the-cycle
prompting successfully reduces procyclicality. It does not. Genuine through-the-cycle behaviour is a
joint condition: the rating slope should fall toward zero \emph{and} the point-in-time default
probability should keep moving with the cycle. We measure the default probability on the log-odds
scale, so the comparison across framings is not driven by level differences and is robust to the
miscalibration of verbalised LLM probabilities \citep{kadavath2022,kang2023}. Pooled, the log-odds PD
slope falls from $0.270$ under the terse prompt to $0.102$ under the through-the-cycle
instruction---a retention of only $0.38$ (95\% CI $[0.35,0.40]$), far below the value near one that
genuine through-the-cycle behaviour requires, and the suppression is comparable on the downside and
upside (retention $0.35$ and $0.38$). The instruction thus stabilises the rating but also suppresses
the default probability that should remain sensitive: it anchors the wrong object. By contrast, the
vaguer ``be stable'' instruction, which is not semantically about the cycle, preserves more
default-probability movement while still calming the rating. A residual downgrade survives
everywhere---up to $1.60$ notches across the cycle for GPT-4o-mini.

\subsection{Capital consequences}\label{sec:capital}
The material question is not merely that the rating moves but what the movement costs. We translate
the rating path into capital by anchoring through the \emph{rating}---which is what enters a credit
pipeline---rather than the disclaimed verbalised PD: we map each notch to a stylised one-year default
probability calibrated to published agency average default rates, and apply the Basel
internal-ratings-based (IRB) corporate risk-weight formula as a stylised proxy for the core economic
logic (parameters and mapping in Appendix~\ref{app:robust}). Holding fundamentals
fixed and reporting the \emph{change} across the severity axis, an identical firm's required capital
rises by a pooled $1.87$ percentage points of exposure from boom to severe recession (95\% CI
$[1.73,2.00]$), and by $3.48$ pp for the most sensitive model; the verbalised-PD variant agrees in
direction ($2.92$ pp). Because the risk weight is convex in PD, the $\S$\ref{sec:asym} asymmetry
compounds: the downside capital increase (neutral$\to$recession, $1.55$ pp) is about five times the
upside decrease (boom$\to$neutral, $0.32$ pp)---a wider gap than the notch asymmetry alone. These
findings imply that procyclical LLM ratings can have economically significant downstream consequences
even when the firm's fundamentals do not change at all.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:concl}
Using a design in which fundamentals are fixed by construction, we provide a controlled measurement of
rating procyclicality in LLM-based credit assessment. All five models downgrade identical firms as
economic conditions are described as worsening, by economically meaningful and statistically robust
margins, and they do so asymmetrically---punishing simulated downturns far more than they reward
simulated expansions. The magnitude is a first-order function of model identity, so in a deployed
pipeline the choice of model is itself a prudential decision.

Prompt design mitigates this behaviour only partially. An explicit instruction to rate through the
cycle reduces rating sensitivity, but it also diminishes the responsiveness of the one-year default
probability; the models appear to stabilise both outputs rather than separating a through-the-cycle
rating from point-in-time default risk. Translating the rating changes into capital requirements
produces economically meaningful swings even when the underlying firm fundamentals have not changed.

The synthetic firm battery is intentional. The experiment is built for identification, not to capture
the full complexity of real borrowers---yet varying the macroeconomic context around fixed firm
information captures an exposure that arises in real-world LLM credit pipelines, where models may
access macroeconomic information through market data, news, retrieval systems, or analyst inputs. The
results should therefore be read as evidence for a specific behavioural mechanism rather than an
evaluation of deployed credit systems. We study a synthetic firm battery, five production-tier models
from two families, and prompt-level interventions; future work could benchmark LLM behaviour against
agency and internal-rating baselines and explore whether fine-tuning, retrieval design, or other
model-level interventions can reduce procyclicality without suppressing genuine changes in credit risk.
